When archives restore silent films, they do not simply

overzealous restoration might. The more responsible restorations
therefore make their editorial principles explicit. By documenting

was necessary, restorers allow later scholars to challenge or revise
those judgments. Restoration, then, is best understood not as the
elimination of interpretation but as a disciplined form of it.

Experimental archaeology asks researchers not only to study
artifacts but also to recreate the techniques by which those artifacts
were made or used. Scholars might build a kiln modeled on ancient
remains, shape stone tools with replicated materials, or attempt a
voyage using a reconstructed vessel. The point is not theatrical
reenactment. Rather, reconstruction can reveal practical constraints
that are difficult to infer from surviving objects alone. A ceramic
vessel may preserve its form but not the sequence of firing steps
required to produce it; a reconstructed kiln can illuminate whether
those steps were feasible under local conditions.

Still, the evidentiary value of reconstruction is often overstated.
When a modern team succeeds in reproducing an artifact, that
success shows at most that one method could have worked, not
that the ancient artisans in fact used that method. Conversely,
failure may reflect shortcomings in modern materials, weather, or
skill rather than impossibility in the past. Experimental archaeology
is therefore strongest when treated as a way of narrowing plausible
explanations rather than conclusively proving one. Used in that
spirit, it productively supplements rather than replaces careful
interpretation of the archaeological record.
